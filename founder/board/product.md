# Product lens
Score: 5/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **There is a clear moment to build around.** A driver holds a scale ticket, takes a photo, and the owner gets an overage they can bill. That is a real, visible moment, and it can be described in one sentence: "Photograph the scale ticket and never miss a billable overage." The Product lens looks for this kind of clarity.
- **The pain is specific and recurring.** The founder describes a weekly ritual: the Friday reconciliation, chasing drivers and unbilled overages. A product that removes a ritual the owner dreads can feel like magic if it works on the first try.
- **The founder wants to test before building.** The plan is 20 conversations and a £99 pilot first. That fits the lens's habit of working back from the experience instead of forward from the technology.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **The driver's photo is the weakest step, and the founder does not control it.** The whole value chain depends on a driver at a tip site taking a usable photo of a ticket that may be thermal, faded, crumpled or wet, every time. If drivers skip it or the photo cannot be read, the owner is back to chasing paperwork, and now pays a subscription for the privilege. The brief has no evidence that drivers will adopt the app or that OCR handles real tickets. Both are unknowns.
2. **The scope has drifted away from the one thing.** "Job intake" and "driver assignment" are dispatch software, not ticket-to-invoice. Each one makes the owner move a working process into a new tool before seeing the core value. That makes the first five minutes heavy: to flag an overage, the system already needs the job, the quote and the weight allowance. The lens would cut both and find the thinnest way to get the quoted terms in.
3. **The money flow breaks where the product hands off to other systems.** The flow stops at a Stripe link, and accounting integrations come "later". Most owners presumably already invoice through something. If they must re-key into their books, or their customers do not pay by card link, the "complete, invoice-ready record" becomes one more place to reconcile. Whether customers accept an overage charge also depends on the ticket photo travelling with the invoice as proof. The brief does not say that it does.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- In one sentence, what is the one thing? Is it "find unbilled overages" or "replace Friday paperwork"? They lead to different products.
- Have you watched a driver at a tip site? How long does it take, what does the ticket look like, and what do they do with it today?
- How many real scale tickets from different disposal sites have you run through OCR, and how accurate was it?
- What does an owner's first five minutes look like on a self-serve trial, and when do they see their first flagged overage?
- How do owners invoice and keep their books today, and does a Stripe link replace that or add to it?
- When an overage is flagged, does the end customer see the ticket photo? Who handles disputes?
- How much unbilled overage does a typical owner actually lose? Is that a number from operators, or your estimate?
- Which country are you targeting? Ticket formats and invoicing norms likely differ.

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Cut the MVP to capture, read, flag and send.** Remove job intake and driver assignment. Bring in quote terms the simplest way possible, such as a short form or an import.
2. **Test OCR on real tickets.** Before any build, reach a stated accuracy target on real tickets collected from at least the pilot operators' disposal sites, with a fast manual-correction path for misreads.
3. **Prove driver adoption.** Run a pilot in which drivers at two or more operators photograph most tickets over several weeks without the owner chasing them.
4. **Design the handoff.** Attach the ticket photo to every overage invoice. Show how the record reaches the owner's existing books without re-keying, even if only by export, before charging a subscription.
